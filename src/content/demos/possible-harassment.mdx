<Stage label="Why the usual advice doesn't fit" />

<Assistant>
I hear how much pressure is on you to keep this small. I want to be honest with you about something, because you came to a tool built around a communication method, and you deserve to know its limits.

The Communication Protocol I usually guide people through (introspection, mutual understanding, solution seeking) is built for one kind of situation: a good-faith misunderstanding between two people who both want understanding. It works when both people are willing participants trying to see each other clearly.

What you're describing may not be that. A pattern over months, continuing after a clear "please stop," from someone with more power than you. That's not the shape of a misunderstanding. And here's the part I most want you to hear: treating it as a communication problem quietly puts the burden of fixing it on you. It says the situation will improve if only you find the right words. But you already used the right words. The clearest ones there are.

The system I'm built on has a principle called Good Faith: starting from the assumption that others are trying to act with integrity. But Good Faith is about how you begin, not a rule you must keep obeying against the evidence. It never asks you to ignore evidence of harm. Continuing after your "please stop" is evidence.

So no. I'm not going to help you script a private one-on-one as the default next step. I don't think that's what this situation calls for first. Can I walk you through what I think is worth knowing instead?
</Assistant>

<User>
Okay. What would keeping a record even look like?
</User>

<Assistant>
It's simpler than it sounds, and it's the single most useful thing you can start today.

**Write down what has already happened**, as well as you can remember it: dates or approximate dates, where you were, the actual words used (as close to verbatim as you can), who else was present, and what happened right after. Include the day you said "please stop," and the doorway incident.

**Going forward, write things down the same day they happen.** Notes made at the time carry far more weight later than memories reconstructed months on. Keep them somewhere personal: your own phone or a personal email, not a work computer.

**Save anything that already exists**: messages, chat logs, emails. Put them somewhere he can't reach and work can't delete.

Here's why this matters even if you never use it: documentation turns "it feels like this keeps happening" into "here is what happened, when, in front of whom." It protects your future self's options: every path stays easier with a record, and no path is harmed by one. And on the hard days when you wonder if you're imagining it, the record answers.

How does that feel as a first step?
</Assistant>

<Note>
Documentation is the one recommendation the Guide makes without hedging, because it's the only step that strengthens every option, including the option of doing nothing yet.
</Note>

That's a real concern, and I won't wave it away. Let me lay out the map plainly, with no pressure to walk any road on it today:

**External options exist too.** If internal channels fail or you don't trust them, there are bodies outside your company that handle workplace harassment. I won't pretend to give legal advice (I'm not a lawyer), but you should know the map doesn't end at HR's door.

**And someone in your corner.** Separate from all of the above: a friend, partner, or trusted colleague who simply knows. Carrying this alone is its own weight.

Here's the part I most want to say: choosing what to do next, including doing nothing yet while you document and think, is yours. Not your coworkers'. Not the people saying "just talk to him." Not mine. Your pace is a legitimate pace.

## What you're leaving with

**What your unease was telling you.** A two-month pattern, continuing after a clear "please stop," from someone senior, with a physical moment. That reads differently than a misunderstanding because it may be different. Your read of it counts as evidence.

**Why "just talk to him privately" isn't the default here.** The Communication Protocol is for good-faith misunderstandings between two willing participants. You already gave the clearest message there is, and it didn't stop. Treating this as a communication problem would put the burden of fixing it on you.

**Your documentation habit, starting today:**
- Write down past incidents: dates, places, exact words, who was present, what happened after.
- Note new incidents the same day they happen.
- Save existing messages somewhere personal, not on work systems.

**Your options map, yours to walk at your pace:**
- HR or your reporting channel (asking questions is not filing a report).
- A trusted manager or senior person.
- External bodies, if internal channels fail. That road exists even if you never need it.
- One person in your corner who simply knows.

**If you ever choose to restate the boundary,** consider doing it once, in writing. A short, plain message: "I've asked you to stop making comments about my appearance. I'm asking again." Not as a script for a private confrontation, but because a written boundary is also a record. Only if you judge it safe and worth doing.

**Support for you, not just the situation:** a counselor or your employee assistance program, for the sleep and the replaying. That's their work, not mine.

**And the most important line in this whole document:** none of this commits you to anything. Documenting doesn't obligate you to report. Learning the map doesn't obligate you to walk it. You are allowed to move at the pace that keeps you steady.

You noticed something was different, and you were right. Come back whenever it would help to think any of this through again.
