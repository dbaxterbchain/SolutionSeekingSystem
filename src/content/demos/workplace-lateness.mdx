<Assistant>
The setting matters as much as the words: private, unhurried, and not in the heat of the moment, so not at 9:16 as he's walking in. Ask for fifteen minutes later that day.

For the opening, the goal is to state what you've observed and what it affects, then hand him the floor before drawing any conclusions. Something like:

> "Marcus, I want to talk about the morning handoffs. You've been late four times this month, and when that happens someone else has to cover your updates. Before anything else, I want to understand what's going on for you. Is there something making mornings hard right now?"

Notice the shape: observable facts, the impact, and then a genuine question. No verdict. How does that sit with you? Is there anything in it that doesn't sound like something you'd actually say?
</Assistant>

<Assistant>
Great question. Vague reassurance is one of the most common ways these conversations stall. Your job then is to gently stay curious rather than accept the deflection or escalate. A few questions to keep in your pocket:

<Stage step={3} label="Solution Seeking" />

<Assistant>
Then you've reached the best possible position: a real problem, jointly understood, with no villain. Now you build a solution together, and the system asks that a solution be **actionable, testable, effective, and time-bound**, and fair to everyone it touches.

Some shapes it could take, depending on what he says:

- **Shift his slot:** Marcus gives his update last, buying him ten minutes.
- **Async fallback:** if he'll be late, he posts his update in the channel by 8:50 so nobody has to cover blind.
- **Adjust the schedule:** if drop-off makes 9:00 permanently tight, maybe his day officially starts at 9:15 with an adjusted end time, *if* that's something you could offer anyone else in his position.

That last condition is your fairness concern doing its job: whatever you agree to should be something Priya could look at and call reasonable. Which of those feels most workable in your team's reality?
</Assistant>

<Assistant>
By making the agreement testable and time-bound instead of a vague promise. For example:

**The agreement:** For the next two weeks, Marcus posts his handoff update in the channel by 8:50 on any day he'll miss 9:00, and his live slot moves to the end of the meeting. **The test:** zero handoffs where someone else has to cover him unprepared. **The review:** you two check in at the end of the two weeks: did it work, did it feel fair, does anything need adjusting?

The review date is the piece most people skip, and it's what makes this a solution instead of a truce. It also gives Marcus a reason to be honest now: he knows there's a real checkpoint, not a warning that fades.
