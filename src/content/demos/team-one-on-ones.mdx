<Stage label="Reframing the purpose" />

<Assistant>
Hold the template a moment longer, because the reframe matters more than the format. In the Solution Seeking System, a one-on-one is a relationship-building basic: it's how you **listen for problems people may not be aware of yet, or are afraid to raise**, and how you find out what you can do to better serve the people you lead. It's proactive listening — leadership as service and guardianship, not extraction.

That means two things. First, the meeting is *theirs*, not yours: they lead, you listen and encourage. Second, status has to physically leave the room, or it will creep back and eat everything — status is what people reach for when a silence gets uncomfortable.

So: where else could ticket status live for your team? Do you already have a standup or a channel that could absorb it?
</Assistant>

<User>
We have a daily standup and the sprint board is right there in Jira, so honestly the status portion was always redundant. Okay, I can buy the reframe. But if we're not going through tickets, what do we even talk about for thirty minutes? I picture a lot of awkward silence.
</User>

<Assistant>
That fear is the most common one, and the answer is a small set of open questions you trust. The tool suggests several, and each one is really a Wisdom Principle wearing conversational clothes:

- **"How have you been doing since our last one-on-one?"** — this is **Understanding** at work. It's deliberately about them, not their tickets.
- **"Are things going smoothly with everyone at work?"** — asked in **Good Faith**: you're assuming friction, if it exists, is misunderstanding rather than malice, and you're offering to hear about it before it hardens.
- **"What do you feel passionate about right now? Is there anything we can do to help you pursue that?"** — service, made concrete.
- **"Do you feel well supported by the team?"**
- **"Do you have any feedback for me, or anything on your mind?"** — this one is **Vulnerability**, and it's yours to model. Answering it honestly takes **Bravery** on their side, which is exactly why you thank people when they do.

You don't ask all five every time. Two or three, then let them lead. Which of these could you imagine saying in your own voice — and which feel like a costume?
</Assistant>

<Stage label="Designing the practice" />

<Assistant>
Do the math before you accept anyone's "non-negotiable." Twelve people at thirty minutes weekly is six hours of one-on-ones every week — plus prep and note follow-up, call it a full day. Meetings you can't sustain become meetings you silently cancel, and a silently cancelled one-on-one teaches your team the practice was theater after all.

The tool's rule of thumb is monthly, and for a team of twelve that's a defensible baseline: roughly ninety minutes a week of listening, which you can protect indefinitely. The trade-off is real — a month is a long gap for someone new or someone struggling — so cadence doesn't have to be uniform. Frequency should follow need, not rank.

What does your calendar honestly have room for, and who on the team needs more frequent attention right now?
</Assistant>

<Assistant>
Monthly baseline with biweekly for the two juniors is a sound design — that's about two and a quarter hours a week, still protectable, weighted toward the people most likely to sit on a problem quietly.

As for making it weird: the way to avoid weird is to be plain. In the system's language, **Clarity is Kindness** — and it applies to the meeting itself, not just what's said inside it. A relaunch that works looks like:

- **Name the change honestly.** Tell the team what you told me: the old one-on-one duplicated standup, so status is going back to standup and the board, permanently.
- **Say what the meeting is now, in ordinary words.** The tool offers language for this: "This is time for us to just chat. We can talk about whatever you want — it's not about anything in particular, just making sure we understand each other."
- **Schedule ahead, as an ask, not a demand.** A regular, predictable slot on the calendar means nobody wonders what they did wrong when the invitation lands. Consent to talk matters.
- **Then let the first meetings prove it.** No announcement convinces a skeptical senior engineer. Acting on the first real thing someone tells you will.

That last point is where relaunches live or die, so let me ask: when something does come up in one of these — a concern, a friction, a request — what do you plan to do with it?
</Assistant>

You're not mining for problems; you're keeping a door unlocked. Trust arrives on its own schedule, usually a few quiet meetings later than you'd like.

<Stage label="The cheat-sheet" />

<Assistant>
## One-on-ones cheat-sheet

### Purpose

A one-on-one is proactive listening — a relationship-building basic. It exists to catch problems people may not be aware of yet or are afraid to raise, and to find out how you can better serve the people you lead. It is **their** space; they lead, you listen. Status lives in standup and the board — never here.

### Cadence

- **Monthly, 30 minutes, everyone** — about 90 minutes a week across twelve people; protectable indefinitely.
- **Biweekly for the two juniors** while they ramp; drop to monthly when they say they're ready.
- Scheduled ahead at a predictable rhythm, as an ask, not a summons — nobody should wonder what they did wrong.

### Opening questions

Two or three per meeting, then follow their lead:

- "How have you been doing since our last one-on-one?" — **Understanding**
- "Are things going smoothly with everyone at work?" — **Good Faith**
- "What are you actually excited about lately? Anything I can do to clear a path?" — service, in your own voice
- "Do you feel well supported by the team?"
- "Do you have any feedback for me, or anything on your mind?" — **Vulnerability** (yours to model; answering takes their **Bravery** — thank them when they do)

### Listening posture

- Notebook out. Write down concerns and anything you promise.
- If something concerning surfaces, don't solve it on the spot — use the Communication Protocol: Introspection to find the right questions, Mutual Understanding to see how they feel and why, then a solution you actually apply and follow up on.
- Thank them genuinely for being brave and vulnerable enough to share. It costs energy.

### What NOT to do

- No status. If it creeps back in, send it back to the channel by name.
- Don't bring your own agenda or pre-existing concerns unless they raise them — this meeting is never an ambush.
- Don't treat it as performance management or surveillance; the moment it becomes evidence-gathering, it dies.
- Don't pad a quiet meeting. Ending early is fine; cancelling silently is not.

### Relaunch plan

- Announce the change plainly to the whole team: status moves to standup permanently; the one-on-one becomes their time. Clarity is Kindness.
- Open the first round with the tool's framing: "This is time for us to just chat, about whatever you want — just making sure we understand each other."
- Act visibly on the first real thing anyone raises. Follow-through is the only argument cynics accept.

### Review the practice itself

- Put a date on the calendar **eight weeks out** to review the practice like any other solution.
- Signals to check: are cancellations down, has anyone raised something that wasn't a status item, did every noted concern get acted on and closed?
- Adjust cadence and questions based on what you find — the practice should evolve with the team.

Take the Dana conversation to the Guide at /practice/guide before her next one-on-one, so that channel stays clean. And good luck with the relaunch — come back after the eight-week review and tell me what the team taught you.
